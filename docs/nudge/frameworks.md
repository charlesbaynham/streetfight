# Behaviour-change frameworks — the working set

Four frameworks, in the order they are usually reached for. Each entry quotes
the primary source where the exact wording matters, because these get
paraphrased into mush everywhere else and the paraphrases lose the caveats.

Fetched and checked against primary sources on 2026-08-31.

---

## EAST — the practitioner's checklist

**Source.** *EAST: Four Simple Ways to Apply Behavioural Insights*, Behavioural
Insights Team, 2014 (Owain Service, Michael Hallsworth, David Halpern et al.).
Executive summary quoted verbatim below from
<https://transparencyschool.org/wp-content/uploads/EAST_ExecSummary.pdf>;
publication page at
<https://www.bi.team/publications/east-four-simple-ways-to-apply-behavioural-insights/>.

> If you want to encourage a behaviour, make it Easy, Attractive, Social and
> Timely (EAST).

### 1. Make it Easy

- **Harness the power of defaults.** "We have a strong tendency to go with the
  default or pre-set option, since it is easy to do so. Making an option the
  default makes it more likely to be adopted."
- **Reduce the 'hassle factor' of taking up a service.** "The effort required
  to perform an action often puts people off. Reducing the effort required can
  increase uptake or response rates."
- **Simplify messages.** "Making the message clear often results in a
  significant increase in response rates to communications. In particular, it's
  useful to identify how a complex goal can be broken down into simpler, easier
  actions."

*Cited example:* auto-enrolment into pension schemes took participation in
large firms from 61% to 83% in six months.

### 2. Make it Attractive

- **Attract attention.** "Ways of doing this include the use of images, colour
  or personalisation."
- **Design rewards and sanctions for maximum effect.** "Financial incentives
  are often highly effective, but alternative incentive designs — such as
  lotteries — also work well and often cost less."

*Cited example:* putting a photograph of the offending vehicle in car-tax
letters moved payment from 40% to 49%.

### 3. Make it Social

- **Show that most people perform the desired behaviour.** "Describing what
  most people do in a particular situation encourages others to do the same.
  Similarly, policy makers should be wary of inadvertently reinforcing a
  problematic behaviour by emphasising its high prevalence."
- **Use the power of networks.** Behaviours spread peer-to-peer.
- **Encourage people to make a commitment to others.** "The social nature of
  these commitments is often crucial."

*Cited example:* HMRC letters saying most people pay on time moved payment
rates by up to 5 percentage points.

### 4. Make it Timely

- **Prompt people when they are likely to be most receptive.** "Behaviour is
  generally easier to change when habits are already disrupted, such as around
  major life events."
- **Consider the immediate costs and benefits.** "We are more influenced by
  costs and benefits that take effect immediately than those delivered later."
- **Help people plan their response to events.** "There is a substantial gap
  between intentions and actual behaviour. A proven solution is to prompt
  people to identify the barriers to action, and develop a specific plan to
  address them."

*Cited example:* a text message ten days before bailiffs are sent doubled the
value of fine payments.

**The caveat BIT itself prints.** Halpern's foreword says outright that EAST
"won't turn you into the world's leading expert" and that "there are more
complex frameworks and typologies, and many subtle and fascinating effects
that EAST does not cover." Treat it as a checklist for generating candidates,
not as a theory of why anything works.

**Ordering matters.** The four are not equally strong. See `evidence.md`:
*Easy* — and defaults in particular — is the only limb with reliably large
effects. Attractive/Social/Timely are real but small and highly
context-dependent. Reach for E first, and reach for A/S/T only once E is
exhausted.

## MINDSPACE — the fuller checklist behind EAST

**Source.** *MINDSPACE: Influencing behaviour through public policy*, Institute
for Government / Cabinet Office, 2010 (Dolan, Hallsworth, Halpern, King,
Vlaev). Definitions quoted verbatim from
<https://www.instituteforgovernment.org.uk/sites/default/files/publications/MINDSPACE.pdf>
(p. 8).

> we set out nine of the most robust (non-coercive) influences on our
> behaviour, captured in a simple mnemonic – MINDSPACE – which can be used as
> a quick checklist when making policy.

| | Effect | The report's own gloss |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **M** | Messenger | "we are heavily influenced by who communicates information" |
| **I** | Incentives | "our responses to incentives are shaped by predictable mental shortcuts such as strongly avoiding losses" |
| **N** | Norms | "we are strongly influenced by what others do" |
| **D** | Defaults | "we 'go with the flow' of pre-set options" |
| **S** | Salience | "our attention is drawn to what is novel and seems relevant to us" |
| **P** | Priming | "our acts are often influenced by sub-conscious cues" |
| **A** | Affect | "our emotional associations can powerfully shape our actions" |
| **C** | Commitments | "we seek to be consistent with our public promises, and reciprocate acts" |
| **E** | Ego | "we act in ways that make us feel better about ourselves" |

**Health warning on P.** *Priming* is the one entry on this list that the
replication crisis went through hardest — social/behavioural priming effects
of the kind in the 2010 literature have largely failed to replicate. Defaults,
norms and salience have held up far better. Do not build a plan on priming.

MINDSPACE is applied through what the report calls the **6 Es**: Explore,
Enable, Encourage, Engage, Exemplify, Evaluate — the middle four from DEFRA's
existing framework, with *Explore* before and *Evaluate* after. The point of
the bookends is that a nudge is a hypothesis, not a fact.

## COM-B — the diagnostic, not a list of tricks

**Source.** Michie, van Stralen & West, "The behaviour change wheel: A new
method for characterising and designing behaviour change interventions",
*Implementation Science* 6:42 (2011).
<https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/1748-5908-6-42>

Any behaviour is the product of three things, each of which can be the binding
constraint:

- **Capability** — the person's psychological and physical capacity to do it:
  knowledge, skill, attention, dexterity. *Do they know how? Can their hands
  do it?*
- **Opportunity** — everything outside the person that makes it possible:
  physical (time, tools, signal, battery, a phone that works in sunlight) and
  social (is it normal here? is anyone watching?).
- **Motivation** — everything in the brain that energises and directs, "not
  just goals and conscious decision-making" but "habitual processes, emotional
  responding, as well as analytical decision-making."

The nine **intervention functions** in the wheel's middle ring: Education,
Persuasion, Incentivisation, Coercion, Training, Restriction, Environmental
Restructuring, Modelling, Enablement.

**Why it earns its place.** EAST tells you what to *do*; COM-B tells you
whether you have diagnosed the right *problem*. Most failed nudges are
motivation interventions aimed at a capability or opportunity problem —
exhorting someone harder to do a thing their thumb cannot physically reach.
Diagnose with COM-B before prescribing from EAST.

## Fogg Behaviour Model — B = MAP

**Source.** BJ Fogg, Stanford Behavior Design Lab; first published at
Persuasive Technology 2009, expanded in *Tiny Habits* (2019).
<https://behaviormodel.org/>

**Behaviour happens when Motivation, Ability and a Prompt converge at the same
moment.** Miss any one and nothing happens.

The three failure modes, and what each looks like in a UI:

| Missing | Symptom | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Prompt** | People who wanted to do it never did | Put the trigger where the person already is, at the moment it applies |
| **Ability** | People start and drop out mid-flow | Remove steps, remove typing, remove decisions |
| **Motivation** | People see it, can do it, decline | Rarest of the three in practice; hardest to fix |

**The load-bearing insight, and the reason this model is worth more than its
pop-science reputation:** raising Ability beats raising Motivation, almost
always. Motivation fluctuates hour to hour and cannot be relied on at the
moment the prompt lands. Simplicity is stable.

Fogg's five dimensions of Ability are a useful checklist for "what is
expensive about this step?": **time, money, physical effort, mental effort
(brain cycles), social deviance, and departure from routine.** In a phone app
at a game night, *mental effort* and *time* dominate; *social deviance* is
real too — a step that requires standing still fiddling with a phone while
other people watch is genuinely costly.

## How the four fit together

```
COM-B          →  what is actually stopping this?      (diagnosis)
Fogg B=MAP     →  is it prompt, ability or motivation? (localise the gap)
EAST           →  candidate interventions, E first     (prescription)
MINDSPACE      →  a longer list when EAST runs dry     (prescription)
```
